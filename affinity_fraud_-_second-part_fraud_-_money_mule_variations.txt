The tactic described is known broadly as an affinity fraud tool, social proof manipulation, or a variant of a mule/intermediary scam. When a fraudster tricks an innocent person into validating a lie to target someone else, they are exploiting human-to-human trust by turning the initial individual into an unwitting accomplice.

Depending on the context, this strategy plays out across several specific schemes:

1. Second-Party Fraud & Money Mule Variations

In second-party fraud, a person is drawn into a scheme—sometimes intentionally, but often through manipulation. For example, in romance scams or fake job scams, a criminal builds intense trust with Victim A. They then ask Victim A to accept funds into their bank account and forward them to a "business partner" (Victim B) or to tell Victim B that the operation is legitimate. Because Victim A genuinely believes the scammer, their passionate, honest corroboration completely disarms Victim B's suspicions. [1] (https://www.alloy.com/guides/fraud-types-guide)

2. Triangulation and Classified Scams

In peer-to-peer marketplaces, scammers use this tactic to manipulate transactions:
• The "Accidental Deposit" Trick: The scammer tricks Person A into receiving a fraudulent check or payment transfer. The scammer then convinces Person A that it was a mistake and asks them to send the cash to Person B (the true target) to pay for a high-value item. Person A unknowingly provides the "proof of payment" that tricks Person B into releasing their merchandise.

3. Astro-Turfing and "Shill" Exploitation

Scammers frequently hijack the accounts of trusted individuals to target their friends and family network.
• Account Takeovers: Once a fraudster hacks into one individual's social media, they message their contacts. The primary victim's account is used to provide glowing corroboration for fake cryptocurrency investments or grant schemes. Because the message comes from a trusted friend, secondary targets bypass their normal skepticism. Legal Definitions

From a legal standpoint, if the initial individual is truly tricked and has no criminal intent, they are legally considered a victim rather than a co-conspirator. However, the overarching act committed by the orchestrator falls squarely under criminal fraud and theft by deception, as they engineered a false statement of material fact specifically to manipulate someone's justifiable reliance. [1] (https://beresfordlaw.com/how-to-prove-fraud/), [2] (https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/fraud)
If you are dealing with a situation where a scammer is attempting this, you can report the details directly to the FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) or through the Federal Trade Commission Fraud Portal.
